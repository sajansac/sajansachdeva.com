Over the last four or five years, I've tried some format of improving myself, or the so-called 75 Hard Challenge. I've terribly failed at it, but I learned a few things along the way.

## Consistency pays off, but not when you stack everything

First, I totally acknowledge that consistency pays off. If you do something daily, you get better at it.

But what happens if you stack up too many things and try to be consistent at all of them? You block your time up. I don't know if it's true for everyone, but individuals like me thrive on doing creative stuff as well. When I stack up too many things daily, my creative energy forces me to stop those mechanical activities, take a break and do something more natural. I have these energies inside me that want to take a step back, think about things, and then do things at my own pace.

Over the years, I have ridiculed myself for not being consistent about all these mechanical affairs: working out daily, reading daily, journaling daily and meditating daily. All of that is fine. You should have a provision to do those things daily.

But I also have a full-time job, I'm the founder of a startup, and I have a disability. That gives me very little empty space. Things get too mechanical to feel good.

## When you feel inspired, hunt

Instead of putting 10 minutes into an activity daily, I benefit from sprinting through it when I get inspired. Naval Ravikant says that whenever you feel inspired, you should work like a lion. When you feel inspired, just go hunt. That's it.

When I look at the times I've made a lot of progress, it didn't happen when I stretched a task and split it into days. If I want to read a book, I will not read 10 or 20 pages today and 10 or 20 pages tomorrow. That's just not going to happen. If I like a book, if I want to read about a topic, I will do it that very day.

## Build capacity

That means I need to build capacity, so that whenever I feel inspired, I can take it up. Having capacity means having the free time, energy and resources at your disposal to do whatever the heck you want to do.

Physically, that means being active. Some things do require discipline, and being physically active is one of them. If it can become a part of your routine, why not? Being active means that whenever I feel inspired to read a book, dive deep into something or build a product, I can go. And if I skip the gym that day because I'm so deeply focused, I want to enjoy it and see it through.

Mentally, it means staying present and stress-free. I continue to kick the noise out of my system. I would rather do nothing than be busy with the noise around me.

It has become a norm for people to be very busy and always doing something. I like doing nothing. I have actively cancelled social plans to just do nothing. I don't intend to be rude, but it lets me build that capacity, keep free time at my disposal, and keep the noise out of my system. I don't feel inspired every day. But I should have the energy and time at my disposal to go and hunt it.

## Design around your natural energy

I've tried to build my routine a number of times: wake up at a set time, sleep by 10pm, keep a tight calendar. None of that worked, at least not for me.

What works for me is a question I asked myself some time ago: if my job, social commitments, money and time were no constraints, what would I do in a given day? Or, put another way: when do I have the best energy of the day to do the task of my choice?

The answer is the moment I wake up. I'm not the kind of person who wants to wake up and make a lot of noise. I like to wake up slowly and naturally, go out for a walk and get a coffee. That's when I have the best energy of the day, and I want to do something deep with it. In fact, if I take a shower first, my focus gets disrupted, so I became ruthless with that routine.

I wake up at 7 some days, 8 on others. I go for a short 20-minute run, then 10 minutes of breathing or meditation, whatever I'm feeling that day, or both. I get my coffee, block any light out of my room, and work in the dark with a focus light and my coffee. My phone goes on DND. I use an app called Focus with Friends, and I have a crazy number of socks there now. It's just fun on the side.

I usually start around 8, and by 10 or 11 I've done the work of the day, be it startup work or my day job.

Funnily enough, I initially thought I would spend three or four hours on deep work daily. Then I realised my focus was so damn good that one to two hours on the deep task was enough. Thanks to AI, the speed of work goes up too.

Once I'm done with the most important thing of the day, I get another coffee and go for a walk. After that, I take a shower and go to the office. I take a shower anyway, so don't get me wrong.

## Evenings are for creative work

I love working out, and I asked myself the same question: if I didn't have any constraints, when would I want to work out? The natural answer was late in the evening, because I also want to see the sunset. I finish my work, see the sunset, and once the sun has set, I go out for my workout. I come back with more energy for creative work.

> Mornings are for deep work. Evenings are for creative work.

## No more rules

This is not the routine the 75 Hard narrative sold me. This is not the routine the 5am club sold me. This is the regimen that works for a knowledge worker like myself.

I now deliberately try not to make rules out of my life. These are just a bunch of things that work very, very well for me. I don't want to ignore what my body and my natural energy are telling me. I want to pay heed to it.
